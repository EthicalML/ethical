POLICY BRIEF ON​
POWERING EUROPE’S DIGITAL TRANSFORMATION:​
A ROADMAP TO GREENER DATA CENTERS
Lead authors: Philipp Hacker, Kai Ebert, Nicolas Alder, Vlad C. Coroamă, Ralf Herbrich
Contributors: Bran Knowles, Lynn Kaack, Alejandro Saucedo
The Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) is the world’s longest established professional
society of individuals involved in all aspects of Computing. It annually bestows the ACM A.M. Turing
Award, often popularly referred to as the “Nobel Prize of Computing.” ACM’s Europe Technology
Policy Committee (“Europe TPC”) is charged with and committed to providing sound technical
information to policy makers and the general public in the service of sound public policymaking.
Europe TPC is pleased to provide this policy brief on the 16th of May, proposing five
recommendations for policy makers to enable more efficient and greener data centers in the
European Union.
Executive Summary
International developments in Singapore, California, or Australia and even China, alongside the
German Energy Efficiency Act, indicate a shift toward more proactive and effective strategies in green
data center regulation. They show that mandatory standards and customer-level reporting are both
feasible and advantageous. These measures can drive efficiency gains even in countries with a
substantial data center footprint and ambitions for further expansion. In this context, ACM Europe
identifies several gaps in the current version of the European data center reporting scheme. The table
below summarizes them, together with suggested paths towards their mitigation.

Recommendations​
Data centers serve as the essential infrastructure for modern digital activities, in particular the
resource-intensive needs of state-of-the-art AI applications. As the deployment of AI expands across
various sectors, data centers face increasing pressure to provide adequate computational power.
However, these operations come at a steep environmental cost, including high and very dense energy
consumption, significant water usage for cooling, and the attendant greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions.
Most recently, the Commission’s AI Continent Action Plan has promised a rapid expansion of data
centres within the European Union, including so‑called “gigafactories.” This initiative strengthens the
Union’s strategic and technological autonomy. However, policy‑makers must ensure that construction
and operation comply with strict sustainability standards to remain consistent with the European
Green Deal and the climate‑neutrality target for 2050.
In recent years, policymakers within the European Union (EU) have moved to regulate data centers
more systematically, recognizing that future climate commitments depend on managing this rapidly
expanding sector. The Energy Efficiency Directive (EU 2023/1791, EED) and the Delegated Regulation
(EU 2024/1364) represent two pillars of this regulatory agenda. They aim to impose standardized
reporting mechanisms for large data centers and set the stage for future performance standards. This
short paper offers a succinct examination of the existing EU rules, explores international frameworks
and Germany’s transposition as examples of a more effective approach, and provides policy
recommendations to bolster data center regulation for achieving sustainability goals throughout the
EU and UK.
Problem A: Absence of Binding EU Efficiency Targets​
Despite requiring extensive reporting on energy consumption and associated metrics, the EED and its
Delegated Regulation do not set enforceable limits on data center energy usage or Power Usage
Effectiveness (PUE) levels. Without minimum performance standards, many operators may continue
current practices, leading to minimal improvements in efficiency.
Recommendation 1: Thresholds, Renewable Quotas, Incentivisation Framework
●​ Bringing EU legislation closer to an efficiency model adopted, e.g., in the German
transposition of the EDD would yield more consistent environmental outcomes across
Member States. Similarly, in Singapore, new data centers have to meet a PUE of 1.3 or lower;
in Australia, 1.4 or lower; and in California and China, 1.5 or lower. In the EU, binding PUE
targets could be phased in over a five-year period, using tiered thresholds according to data

center age and capacity. Similarly, mandatory renewable energy quotas would push operators
to accelerate the integration of green energy sources and reduce reliance on fossil fuels.
●​ EU institutions should introduce financial or operational incentives for data centers
outperforming baseline requirements, such as access to cheaper green power contracts. At
the same time, operators could be required to disclose to each customer the specific share of
energy and water usage attributable to that customer’s workloads. AI developers and other
service clients would then be better positioned to differentiate among hosting providers
based on sustainability performance.
●​ AI and data center operators should be required to partially, and increasingly, offset their
growing demand by funding equivalent new renewable sources, preventing the depletion of
green energy for other sectors. In Singapore, for example, such requirements are already part
of data center regulation. In the EU, they could be introduced in a time-staggered fashion
that gives industry time to adapt; as the technology matures, fourth-generation nuclear
reactors can be re-considered for offsetting, too.
●​ Proportionate and workable energy reuse quota, e.g. for excess heat, should be introduced at
the EU level.
Problem B: Incomplete Coverage of Facilities
The current 500 kW threshold (a relatively modest benchmark) still excludes a substantial number of
smaller or mid-sized data centers, which in aggregate may account for considerable energy
consumption. Partial reporting undermines the reliability of overall sustainability assessments. As big
operators are more likely to be transparent, smaller data centers risk slipping under the radar entirely.
​
Recommendation 2: Broaden the Scope to Smaller Data Centers
●​ Lowering the 500 kW threshold to 300 kW—or potentially even lower—would significantly
improve data collection. A tiered approach could impose lighter but still meaningful reporting
obligations on mid-sized or small facilities, thus building a more accurate macro-level picture
of the sector’s total environmental footprint.
Problem C: Limited Water Control Measures
Although operators must report water usage, there is no obligation to actively reduce water
consumption or apply state-of-the-art cooling systems. Countries more prone to droughts may see an
outsized impact, in part due to data centers for AI; this highlights the need for more stringent
water-management standards at the EU level, a topic the Commission is now looking into as part of
its upcoming Water Resilience Strategy.
Recommendation 3: Strengthen Water Efficiency and Usage Targets
●​ Regulations should go beyond mere reporting by establishing benchmarks for water
usage effectiveness (WUE) and incentives for advanced cooling systems. Benchmarks
should reflect direct consumption, but increasingly include indirect consumption (e.g.,
large-scale hydro-power plants) as appropriate and feasible. Again, in Singapore, clear
steps are undertaken to bring data center WUE below 2.0 m3/MWh. In the EU, grants, tax
credits, or other support might spur retrofitting and modernization, thereby reducing
water-intensive cooling methods. This shift would be particularly beneficial in regions
experiencing increasing water scarcity.
●​ The AI Act and the EED should align to make water consumption and indirect GHG effects,
for instance from AI-enabled fossil fuel exploration, subject to mandatory reporting. Clear
thresholds need to be set for the reporting of indirect effects, though.
Problem D: Restricted Public Access to Detailed Data
While aggregated performance indicators become publicly available, the full dataset—including
detailed consumption and efficiency metrics—generally remains inaccessible outside authorized

regulatory channels. Researchers, civil society organizations, and smaller companies thus face
challenges in independently verifying or benchmarking data centers’ sustainability records.
Recommendation 4: Enhance Transparency and Public Availability of Data
●​ A central EU registry, with appropriate data anonymization, should be put in place to
enable granular reporting (server-level PUE, water usage in cubic meters, etc.) while
safeguarding trade secrets. This would allow independent researchers and advocacy
organizations to conduct robust assessments of data centers’ environmental
performance, reinforcing market pressure on operators to innovate.
●​ While estimates may be acceptable as a fallback, actual measurements using existing
hardware instruments should hold primacy. This mitigates the risk of under-reporting or
guesswork.
Problem E: Uncertain Long-Term Roadmap
Given the rapid evolution of high-capacity AI workloads and the ambitious goals in the AI Continent
Action Plan, peak energy demands in data centers may outstrip any temporary gains made through
basic efficiency improvements. Regulatory focus on progressive enhancements—backed by better
enforcement tools—remains lacking at the EU level, even though the European Commission is
required to publish further proposals by 2025.
​
Recommendation 5: Prepare for Future AI Energy Demands
●​ As AI applications continue to expand, data centers require more sophisticated electricity
management strategies, including load shifting and dynamic allocation of workloads to
off-peak times or to sites with surplus renewable energy. Policymakers should plan to
integrate these advanced strategies into the legal framework—particularly to
accommodate the intense load spikes generated by training large AI models or handling
massive inference volumes.
Conclusion
Data centers have emerged as a crucial focus for policymakers seeking to align Europe’s digital
transformation with the EU’s climate ambitions. Despite the progress made through the Energy
Efficiency Directive (EU 2023/1791) and Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1364, the current regime
relies heavily on data disclosure and thus falls short of establishing definitive targets or binding
thresholds. This is all the more important given the announcements in the AI Continent Action Plan.
International developments as well as the German Energy Efficiency Act point to a more proactive
and effective strategy, demonstrating that mandatory standards and customer-level reporting are
both viable and beneficial for driving efficiency gains even in countries with a large data center
presence and significant ambitions for expanding them.
By introducing enforceable efficiency benchmarks, lowering the reporting threshold, strengthening
water-management obligations, and improving transparency, EU regulators can move toward a more
effective framework capable of handling the rapidly escalating demands of AI. Such reforms are not
only possible but urgent, ensuring that Europe’s data centers evolve into more sustainable, resilient
infrastructures commensurate with the digital economy’s long-term needs and the dual
transformations in AI and climate.
