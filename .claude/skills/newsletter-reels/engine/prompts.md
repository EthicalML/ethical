# Prompts that worked

These are the prompts, briefs and feedback that produced the best films in the exercise this skill came from, lightly edited for typos only. They carry more of the craft than any template: use them as creative direction, not as a checklist.

## The bar

Open every film brief, and every subagent brief, with this line:

> To make a motion graphics video that shows what an incredible motion designer you are, like it's your showreel for a global expo keynote. Go all out.

The very first prompt of the exercise, which produced the 15-second showreel everything else grew from, was:

> Make a dynamic 15-second motion graphics video that shows what an incredible motion designer you are, like it's your showreel for a résumé. Go all out.

## Directions

The examples inside each direction come from a pricing deck; translate them into your own story's objects. Pick one direction per film. They are genuinely different films from the same story. If the user hasn't chosen, and you can run subagents, build two or three directions in parallel from the same `story.md` and let the user pick. That's how the winning film was found.

### Connected showreel (the one that won)

> Tell the full story as a self-playing film in the visual language of the first showreel: dark stage, kinetic type slamming in, bold colour fields flipping on the beat, shape morphing, a 3D point field, HUD rings, tesseract-level geometry, grain, motion blur, camera shake and colour fringing on impacts, cut on a 120 BPM grid. Crucially: NO slide transitions. Scenes must be connected. The element that ends one beat becomes the start of the next: the inventory line becomes the demand field; the decision grid's cells scatter into the point cloud; the chosen point rides the frontier and then becomes the product mark; the test dots fly in from the cloud. Design a continuous camera and object narrative first, as a beat map, then build.

### Morph and depth

> Keep the brand's own look (clean deck typography, its colours) but remove every slide transition. Too much was taken verbatim from the slides, and slide-to-slide transitions feel cheap. Every object must transform into the next: the line bends into the next curve, which splits, which extrudes into a 3D surface, which shatters into the point cloud, which is swept by the frontier, whose chosen point becomes the product block. Much more 3D: real perspective camera moves, extruded charts, a voxel field for the decision space, a ridge you fly along, fog and soft light that still read as the brand. Headlines stay crisp and legible.

### Infinite zoom

> Every beat is nested inside a detail of the previous one: zoom into the "0" of "0% markdown" to find the inventory curve, into one point on the curve to find a product, into the product to find the decision grid, into one cell to find the whole cloud, into a point on the frontier to find the product mark. Design the nesting chain first. The zoom must be truly continuous: vector and text re-rendered crisply at every depth, never a scaled-up bitmap.

### One transforming object

> One persistent object (thousands of particles or instanced shapes) lives centre stage for the entire film and is continuously reshaped into each beat's form: a lever, two blocks, a ribbon, a bending curve, a voxel matrix, an exploding cloud, a frontier ridge with a glowing point, a solid product block, the result numerals as extruded forms. The camera orbit changes per beat. Type reveals are tied to the motion too, never plain fade-ins.

### Layered velocities

> The world does not move as one sheet: many layers travel at different speeds AND in different directions, some sideways, some against the motion, some rotating in depth. Pieces of each chart arrive from separate layers and lock together exactly when the beat is centred, then disassemble into the next beat's pieces. Giant outlined words drift slowly behind; price tags ride the curves faster in front.

## Feedback that changed the film the most

Use these as the lens in every review round. Each is a note the user actually gave, and each made the film clearly better.

- "I want all animations to connect to each other, which means that the objects would transform from one to the next, as I do not like the slide transition animations."
- "Too much was taken verbatim [from the slides]. I would like to have much more 3D cool things and things that connect to each other."
- "The bar with the pricing intelligence reveal was really good. I would rather have it more animated and more things like that one, maybe particle effects, or something that is connected." (So: give the best moment an extra bar, and bring its energy into the scenes that follow.)
- "The end is too long, and it's too boring." (So: the ending must be alive, with particles assembling and collapsing, never a held lock-up.)
- "A bit more pause before the closing line, as it's too sudden." (So: give the ending a bar to breathe.)
- On the music: "the drums are too dry." And after an A/B test: "yes, that's more what I meant." That approved the punchy kit.

## The review prompt

After every build, review like this, and do at least three rounds:

> Capture frames across the whole film and at every scene hand-off, build a contact sheet, look at it, and iterate on anything broken, overlapping, illegible or dull. Treat any held frame with nothing alive, any hand-off that doesn't grow out of the previous scene, and any number not in the source as a bug.
